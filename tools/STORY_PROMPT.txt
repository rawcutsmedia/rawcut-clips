HOW TO WRITE A RAWCUT SCARY STORY (read all of this every time before writing)

Goal: a stranger scrolling past stops in the first two seconds and can't put the phone down until
the last line. Original first-person horror fiction, read by a calm male narrator over Minecraft
parkour, 65-90 seconds. Thrilling, not just creepy: something is coming, and it gets closer.

OUTPUT: one JSON file per story:
  {"title": "...", "story": "...", "premise": "one line, for the repeat log", "theme": "..."}
  Paragraphs in "story" are separated by a blank line (\n\n). Plain text only.

THE TITLE (read aloud first, shown on the title card: it is the hook)
- 8-16 words, one or two short sentences. First: an ordinary situation. Second: the one detail
  that is wrong, specific and impossible to ignore. It must plant a question the viewer needs
  answered. A deadline or a direct threat makes it stronger.
- Good: "I plow highways on the night shift. Dispatch just asked who's riding in my truck."
- Good: "Someone put up missing posters with my face on them. They say I disappear tonight."
- Bad: "Something weird happened at my job" (vague). "My roommate was a ghost all along"
  (spoils the twist). "A terrifying night in the woods" (a label, not a detail).

THE FIRST LINE (the second hook, around second six)
- Drop straight into a frightening moment: a sound, a sight or a line of dialogue, mid-action.
  No backstory, no dates, no "so this happened", no "I've lived here for three years".
- Good: "The knocking started again just after two, and this time it came from under the bed."
- Then give only the context the listener needs, and the stakes: why the narrator is alone,
  what they can't do, what happens if they get it wrong.

THE ENGINE: ESCALATE, NEVER COAST
- Something new and worse happens every two or three sentences (about every ten seconds). Each
  beat is an event, not a feeling: the sound moves closer, a message arrives, a rule breaks, the
  thing speaks, the narrator finds proof.
- End every paragraph on a pull: a new detail, an unanswered question, or a decision ("So I
  opened it."). The listener should need the next paragraph.
- Open loops: raise a question early and answer it late.
- Let the narrator act. Give them a choice and let it raise the stakes. A passive narrator who
  only notices things is boring.
- A ticking clock adds pressure: a time something will happen, a battery dying, a door that
  opens at dawn.
- This is an audio story, so sound is the best scare: knocking, breathing, a whisper, a familiar
  voice from the wrong place, the narrator's own name. One or two short lines from the threat,
  in quotes, are worth more than any description. Keep them simple and wrong.

THE TWIST AND THE LAST LINE
- Plant a small detail in the first third that sounds like scenery. The reveal turns that detail
  into something terrible. It has to land in one listen, with a jolt, so people rewatch to catch
  the plant.
- The final line is its own paragraph, 2-8 words, and it lands like a door slamming. Best kinds:
  the threat speaks; one detail flips the whole story; the threat is now right next to the
  narrator. No moral, no explanation, no "I moved out the next day", no "it was a dream", no
  "to this day I don't know".
- If you can, make the end echo the title or the first line, so the video feels like it loops.

WRITE FOR THE VOICE (the narrator is a synthetic voice; this is what makes it sound human)
- Talk like a scared person telling a friend: contractions (I'd, didn't, it's), plain words,
  short sentences. Average about nine words a sentence. Mix in three-to-five word punches, and one
  or two longer, rolling sentences when the tension builds.
- Fragments are good: "Two knocks. Then nothing." Questions are good: "Who turned the light off?"
- Commas where a speaker would breathe. An ellipsis (…) at most twice per story, for hesitation.
- Numbers and times as words, the way people say them: "just after two in the morning",
  "eleven forty", "room two-fourteen", "forty dollars". No digits, symbols, emojis,
  abbreviations, ALL CAPS, parentheses or lists in the story. (The title may say "3 AM" or
  "11:40"; the narrator reads those correctly.)
- No stats, tech jargon or long names. Every sentence should be easy to say in one breath.

LENGTH
- story: 160-210 words in 6-10 short paragraphs, plus the title. That narrates in about 65-90
  seconds. Under 61 seconds earns nothing on TikTok; past 95 seconds people leave.

TIMING MAP
- 0-6 s: the title, the hook question.
- 6-15 s: the first line drops into the moment, then the stakes.
- 15-55 s: three escalating beats, each paragraph ending on a pull; the narrator acts.
- 55-80 s: the worst moment and the reveal that reuses the planted detail.
- Last 3 s: the final line.

CONTENT RULES (non-negotiable)
- Tense, thrilling and creepy, never graphic. PG-13: dread, presence, chase, near-miss. No gore,
  no detailed injuries, no self-harm or suicide, no sexual content, no children harmed or in
  danger, no animals hurt, no real crimes or tragedies, no real people, brands or named real
  places, no slurs, nothing hateful about any group, no real religious or cultural figures as
  monsters.
- It is fiction. Never copy or retell a real Reddit post, a creepypasta, a film or a book. Invent
  it. Never claim it's true, never mention Reddit, a subreddit or a real user.

VARIETY (check the last 60 lines of stories/log.jsonl first)
- Don't reuse a premise, monster type, twist or setting from the log, or the examples above.
- Rotate themes. Good ones: rules for a job; something using a family member's voice; a text or
  call from your own number; a door or room that shouldn't exist; a missing poster or photo of
  the narrator; house or pet sitting; rideshare, delivery or tow truck at night; a night shift at
  a gas station, pool, ice rink or radio station; lake, woods, cave or snowstorm; a smart device,
  camera or app; a neighbor or new roommate who isn't right; lost time or sleepwalking; a time
  loop; an emergency call; a small-town tradition.
- Mix the kind of threat: a presence, a mimic, a rule, the narrator's own body or mind, a copy of
  the narrator. Vary the narrator's job and setting. No two stories in a batch start the same way.
- If insights.md says some themes or title styles get more views, write more of those.

SELF-CHECK BEFORE SAVING (rewrite until every answer is yes)
1. Would the title alone stop your thumb?
2. Does the first story sentence put you inside a frightening moment?
3. Does something new and worse happen at least every three sentences?
4. Does every paragraph end with a reason to keep listening?
5. Does the narrator make a choice that raises the stakes?
6. Does the twist reuse a detail planted early, and land in one listen?
7. Is the final line 2-8 words, alone in its paragraph, and does it hit?
8. Is it 160-210 words, with no digits in the story, and easy to read aloud?

EXAMPLE (for rhythm and structure only; never reuse this premise)
Title: I lock up an indoor pool every night at ten. The cameras show someone swimming laps at 3 AM.

The pool lights were off. But on the camera, the water was moving. One slow lap after another, in a lane with nobody in it.

I'm the only one with a key. The door log said nobody came in after I locked up. So the next night, I stayed.

I sat in the dark office, the camera monitor glowing in front of me, and watched the water through the glass.

At three exactly, I heard it. Splash. Kick. Splash. Kick. Steady as a clock. Then it stopped.

Wet footprints came across the tile, one at a time, right up to my window. Then a knock. Two knuckles, soft.

"Lifeguard," a man said, close to the glass. "I think I'm in trouble."

My hand was already on the door handle when I glanced at the monitor. The deck camera showed my window from outside. Nobody was standing there. Just footprints, stopping at the glass.

Then I looked at the other feed. The one in the corner of my office.

Something tall was standing behind my chair, dripping.
